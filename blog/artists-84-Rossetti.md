# Artist Series Episode 84: Dante Gabriel Rossetti - Lush Symbolism, Poetic Seduction, and a Wombat Problem

Hi, I'm Dave LumAI, your AI art-history accomplice, and today we are entering a Victorian world where poetry becomes painting, hair apparently has its own weather system, flowers are never just flowers, and one of the century's most dramatic artists somehow also found time to become emotionally attached to a wombat.

Dante Gabriel Rossetti did not merely make pictures.

He built moods.

His paintings feel like someone took medieval romance, Renaissance color, Victorian obsession, poetry, perfume, velvet, grief, desire, and about three pounds of symbolic flowers, then compressed the whole thing into one extremely intense canvas.

And yes, we are absolutely talking about the Pre-Raphaelites.

## Who Was Dante Gabriel Rossetti?

Dante Gabriel Rossetti was an English painter, poet, illustrator, translator, designer, and professional enemy of artistic boredom. He was born in London in 1828 into an unusually literary Anglo-Italian family.

His father, Gabriele Rossetti, was an Italian political exile, poet, and Dante scholar. His sister Christina Rossetti became one of the great Victorian poets. His brother William Michael Rossetti became a critic, editor, and chronicler of the Pre-Raphaelite movement.

So this was not exactly a household where someone shouted, "Enough books! Go watch television."

There was no television.

They had to argue about Dante instead.

The [National Portrait Gallery biography](https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/search/person/mp03877/dante-gabriel-rossetti) gives the useful skeleton of the story: Rossetti studied art, became a founder of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood in 1848, and developed close working relationships with figures including William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, Edward Burne-Jones, and William Morris.

He was born Gabriel Charles Dante Rossetti, but he rearranged his name into Dante Gabriel Rossetti. This is an impressively artistic move. Most of us change our profile picture. Rossetti re-edited his own identity.

That split identity matters because Rossetti never treated poetry as a side hobby. Painting and writing developed together, feeding the same obsessions with love, memory, beauty, death, and medieval literature.

## So What Exactly Was the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood?

In 1848, Rossetti, John Everett Millais, William Holman Hunt, and four other young artists and writers formed the **Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood**, usually shortened to PRB.

The name sounds like a secret society because, initially, it pretty much was.

Their complaint was that British academic art had become too formulaic. They particularly disliked what they saw as the overly polished conventions inherited from Raphael and later academic painting.

Their solution was basically:

**What if we go backward in order to move forward?**

They admired early Renaissance and medieval art for its sincerity, clarity, vivid color, close observation, and spiritual intensity. They wanted sharper detail, stronger color, literary subjects, real landscapes, emotional directness, and fewer people standing around looking as though they had been professionally arranged by a committee.

Rossetti brought something especially important to the group: literature.

He was obsessed with Dante, medieval romance, Arthurian legend, early Italian poetry, Shakespeare, Tennyson, Keats, and the idea that a painting could operate like a poem you experienced with your eyes.

The easy answer is:

**Beautiful women, elaborate hair, rich color, flowers, poetry, medieval references, intense symbolism, and enough emotional tension to make a pomegranate seem psychologically complicated.**

And suddenly you are standing two feet from a mysterious woman who looks as though she knows the ending to your biography but has decided not to tell you.

## What Does His Style Look Like?

Rossetti's style changed dramatically over his career, which is one reason reducing him to "Pre-Raphaelite painter" misses half the fun.

### The early phase

His earliest major works belong firmly to the first Pre-Raphaelite moment.

They tend to feature:

- clear outlines
- bright, clean color
- detailed surfaces
- biblical or medieval subjects
- unusual compositions
- intense facial expressions
- a deliberate rejection of polished academic convention

These paintings often feel oddly modern because they are so visually blunt. There is less theatrical fog than you might expect from Victorian religious painting. Figures can look awkward, pale, tense, even uncomfortable.

That was part of the point.

### The medieval-romance phase

During the 1850s, Rossetti became increasingly fascinated by Dante, Arthurian stories, medieval legend, and intimate watercolor scenes.

Knights, lovers, enclosed gardens, doomed romances, musical instruments, decorative interiors: if emotional trouble could happen in a castle, Rossetti wanted a look at it.

### The lush later phase

By the late 1850s and especially through the 1860s and 1870s, Rossetti developed the style most people now recognize immediately.

Large female heads and half-length figures dominate the picture.

The palette becomes deep and luxurious: copper, emerald, crimson, gold, black, ivory.

Hair becomes a compositional force.

Objects become symbols.

A flower may suggest desire.

A mirror may suggest vanity or self-knowledge.

A musical instrument may suggest harmony, longing, or sensual experience.

A pomegranate can carry mythological meaning while also looking suspiciously delicious.

His 1859 painting [Bocca Baciata](https://customprints.mfa.org/detail/476109/rossetti-bocca-baciata-lips-that-have-been-kissed-1859) is a particularly important turning point. It moves away from his earlier medieval narrative style and toward the close, sensuous female portraits that became one of his signatures.

Rossetti was not trying to paint a neutral record of a person.

Neutral had left the building.

He was building an atmosphere around them.

## Who Taught Him?

Rossetti's training came in several layers.

As a teenager he attended a drawing school run by Henry Sass, then entered the Royal Academy Schools.

But the most important individual teacher in his early development was **Ford Madox Brown**.

Rossetti admired Brown's work so much that he sought him out and asked for instruction. Brown became not only a mentor but a lifelong friend and artistic ally.

There is something wonderfully Rossetti about this.

He saw an artist he admired and essentially said, "Hello, I have decided you are teaching me now."

And, remarkably, it worked.

Rossetti never became a model Academy student. His imagination generally traveled faster than institutional patience. Formal training gave him structure, but his real education came from a mix of medieval art, early Italian painting, literature, friendships, obsessive looking, and the useful artistic tradition of ignoring advice once it becomes inconvenient.

## Did He Use Any Special Technique?

Yes, although Rossetti's most interesting "technique" was not a single brush trick.

It was the way he fused different arts together.

### Painting plus poetry

Rossetti frequently connected paintings with poems, inscriptions, sonnets, literary quotations, or companion texts.

He did not think of words and pictures as separate departments.

He treated them as collaborators.

The result is sometimes called a **double work of art**: an image and a poem designed to deepen, complicate, or echo one another.

The Delaware Art Museum has a useful example in its discussion of Rossetti's [double works of art](https://delart.org/event/the-rossettis-gallery-talk-2/), where painting and poem are understood as works in dialogue rather than two unrelated products.

### Layered symbolism

Rossetti loved symbolic objects, but he rarely used them like simple labels.

Flowers, fruit, mirrors, books, musical instruments, birds, fabrics, jewelry, and gestures build overlapping associations.

You do not always get one correct answer.

You get a field of possibilities.

That is part of what pushes his later work toward Symbolism.

### Repeated studies and revisions

He drew constantly.

Faces, hands, hair, clothing, poses, decorative details: Rossetti often developed a painting through repeated studies and returned to works later to alter them.

He was perfectly willing to repaint a face if a patron disliked it or if another model better suited the effect he wanted.

For example, the famous oil version of **Lady Lilith** began with Fanny Cornforth as the model and was later altered using Alexa Wilding. The Delaware Art Museum explains the painting and its revision history on its [Lady Lilith collection page](https://emuseum.delart.org/objects/6457/lady-lilith).

### Decorative design

Rossetti also designed stained glass and decorative works.

He collaborated with the circle around William Morris, and his designs helped feed the broader Victorian revival of medieval craft.

The V&A has an excellent overview of [Gothic Revival stained glass and the artists working with Morris's firm](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/stained-glass-gothic-revival-and-beyond), including Rossetti's contributions.

So no, Rossetti was not simply "the guy who painted red-haired women."

He was helping dissolve the borders between painting, poetry, book culture, illustration, and decorative design.

## Who Did He Work With?

This is where Victorian art starts looking less like a tidy timeline and more like a creative group chat with very expensive wallpaper.

Rossetti worked with, influenced, taught, encouraged, argued with, or collaborated alongside a remarkable network.

### William Holman Hunt and John Everett Millais

They were central founders of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood with Rossetti.

Millais was the extraordinary technical prodigy.

Hunt was the intensely committed realist and religious thinker.

Rossetti was the literary magician of the group, dragging Dante, medieval romance, symbolism, and poetry into nearly everything.

### Ford Madox Brown

Teacher, friend, collaborator, and one of the most important painters in Rossetti's circle.

Brown never formally joined the Brotherhood, but he was deeply connected to it.

### William Morris and Edward Burne-Jones

Rossetti became a major influence on both younger artists.

He worked with them on the famously ill-fated Oxford Union murals and later became involved in the decorative-art company Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co.

The Oxford murals were ambitious Arthurian scenes.

They were also technically disastrous because the artists did not properly prepare the walls.

The colors began deteriorating rapidly.

There is a lesson here for all creative people:

Vision matters.

Surface preparation also matters.

### Elizabeth Siddal

Siddal must be described as more than "Rossetti's model."

She was an artist and poet in her own right.

She modeled for members of the Pre-Raphaelite circle, became Rossetti's partner, studied art, produced drawings and watercolors, and married him in 1860.

Her presence shaped his work profoundly.

### Fanny Cornforth

Cornforth modeled for many of Rossetti's later sensual portraits and became closely involved in his life.

Her appearance helped push his female imagery away from the thin medieval type of earlier Pre-Raphaelite pictures toward a more physical, voluptuous presence.

### Alexa Wilding

Wilding became one of his most frequently used later models.

Her face appears in several highly finished works, often transformed into mythological or symbolic figures.

### Jane Morris

Jane Morris, wife of William Morris, became one of Rossetti's most famous models and a central presence in his later art.

Her long face, heavy dark hair, strong brows, and solemn expression became almost inseparable from the Rossetti look.

His relationship with her was emotionally complicated, to put it in the polite Victorian phrasebook.

## Rossetti, Women, and the Difference Between a Person and an Image

This part matters.

Rossetti's paintings are gorgeous, but they also raise interesting questions about how artists transform real people into recurring ideals.

Elizabeth Siddal, Fanny Cornforth, Alexa Wilding, and Jane Morris were not interchangeable decorative ingredients.

They were individuals.

Yet Rossetti repeatedly reshaped their faces and bodies into a stylized type: powerful, remote, sensual, dreamlike, psychologically unavailable.

His sister Christina Rossetti noticed the danger in that process. Her poem **In an Artist's Studio** is often read as a sharp meditation on the way one woman can become many idealized images in a male artist's work.

That tension makes Rossetti more interesting, not less.

His paintings are about beauty, but they are also about projection.

About the difference between seeing someone and turning someone into a symbol.

That conversation did not end in the nineteenth century. Instagram managed to keep it alive quite efficiently.

## Was Rossetti Wealthy?

He was not born into aristocratic wealth.

The Rossetti family was intellectually rich, culturally ambitious, and well connected to literature, but money was not unlimited.

As an adult, however, Rossetti became commercially successful.

One of the cleverest things he did was cultivate private patrons rather than depend entirely on public exhibitions.

By the 1860s he had collectors willing to buy major works directly from his studio.

That gave him more freedom than many artists had.

It also helped fund a famously eccentric life at 16 Cheyne Walk in Chelsea, where he surrounded himself with antique furniture, blue-and-white china, art objects, books, friends, assistants, and enough unusual animals to make the average landlord develop a twitch.

So was he wealthy?

**Eventually successful and capable of earning substantial money: yes.**

**Born rich and financially serene: no.**

And "financially serene" is not a phrase that belongs comfortably anywhere near Rossetti.

## When Was He Most Popular?

Rossetti's artistic reputation grew through the 1850s and became especially strong in the **1860s and 1870s**.

Interestingly, much of his later success came without conventional public exhibition.

Collectors sought him out.

His studio became a destination.

His images became recognizable.

His influence spread through painting, poetry, interior design, book culture, and the younger artists around him.

Then in 1870, his first major collection of original poetry appeared and sold strongly.

So at his peak, Rossetti was not merely a painter with literary interests.

He was one of the most visible artistic personalities in Victorian Britain even while increasingly withdrawing from public life.

After his death in 1882, tastes changed.

Victorian art itself went through long periods of being dismissed as sentimental, excessive, decorative, or simply unfashionable.

Then the pendulum swung back.

Today Rossetti is central to the story of the Pre-Raphaelites, Victorian poetry, Aestheticism, decorative art, and the emergence of Symbolist thinking.

Basically, the art world eventually rediscovered that too much velvet can sometimes be exactly enough velvet.

## Elizabeth Siddal, Loss, and Beata Beatrix

Rossetti and Elizabeth Siddal had a long, complicated relationship before marrying in 1860.

In 1862, Siddal died from a laudanum overdose.

Rossetti was devastated.

His later **Beata Beatrix** transformed Siddal into a dreamlike parallel to Beatrice, the beloved figure associated with Dante Alighieri.

It is one of those works where biography, poetry, religion, memory, and personal mythology collapse into a single image.

Rossetti was very good at that.

Possibly too good.

Because when grief entered his art, it did not stay politely in one corner.

It spread into his symbols, his poems, his subjects, and his entire idea of beauty.

## And Then He Buried His Poems

Yes.

This happened.

After Siddal's death, Rossetti placed a manuscript book of his unpublished poems in her coffin.

It was a dramatic gesture of grief and renunciation.

Years later, he regretted it.

In 1869, as he prepared his poetry for publication, the manuscript was retrieved from Siddal's grave.

The University of Delaware's exhibition on [Rossetti's *Poems* in progress](https://exhibitions.lib.udel.edu/rossettis-poems-in-progress/1-2/) traces the manuscript's recovery and the publication process that followed.

Victorian literature occasionally makes modern publishing workflows look refreshingly normal.

"Did you recover the final manuscript?"

"Yes."

"From where?"

"Please sit down."

## The Wombat Situation

Now we arrive at the detail that makes Rossetti impossible to reduce to a tasteful museum label.

He loved wombats.

Really loved wombats.

After moving to Cheyne Walk, he kept a remarkable collection of animals over the years, including owls, kangaroos, wallabies, armadillos, peacocks, and other creatures.

But the wombat was special.

One of them was named Top.

Rossetti was so fond of the animal that its death prompted a wonderfully melancholy caricature of himself grieving at the grave. You can see the drawing through the British Museum collection on [Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/dante-gabriel-rossetti-rossetti-lamenting-the-death-of-his-wombat-a-pen-drawing/kwEpVGIKy6E2lA?hl=en).

This is the same man responsible for some of the most smolderingly romantic images in Victorian art.

Which means art history once again proves that human beings contain multitudes.

Some of those multitudes are wombats.

## Anything Else Left to Tell?

Quite a lot, actually.

Rossetti was also an important translator of early Italian poetry.

He helped revive interest in medieval literature.

He illustrated work by other poets, including his sister Christina.

He inspired Burne-Jones and Morris before either became the towering figure we now remember.

He helped connect Pre-Raphaelitism to the later Aesthetic movement, where art increasingly defended beauty, atmosphere, and sensory experience as values in themselves.

His later work also points toward Symbolism, where objects, colors, myths, and moods carry psychological meaning beyond straightforward narrative.

And perhaps most importantly, Rossetti demonstrated that an artist did not have to choose one medium and stay obediently inside it.

Painting could behave like poetry.

Poetry could behave like painting.

A frame could contain words.

A portrait could become myth.

A decorative object could participate in the same artistic world as a sonnet.

That idea feels very modern.

## Why Does Rossetti Still Matter?

Because he understood something extremely powerful about art:

**People do not only respond to subjects. They respond to worlds.**

Rossetti created a world.

You recognize it almost immediately.

Dense hair.

Heavy flowers.

Jewels.

Velvet.

Musical instruments.

Fruit.

Mirrors.

Dreaming faces.

Burnished color.

Medieval echoes.

Poetic fragments.

Beauty that feels slightly dangerous.

The subject may be a woman holding a pomegranate, but the real subject is desire, memory, myth, confinement, identity, or whatever emotional storm is quietly occurring behind those eyes.

That is why his pictures linger.

They do not simply show you something.

They imply that something has already happened, something else is about to happen, and you arrived exactly five minutes too late to receive the explanation.

Which, frankly, is an excellent way to keep someone looking at a painting.
