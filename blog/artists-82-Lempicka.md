# Episode 82: Tamara de Lempicka, or How to Make Art Deco Look Like It Has a Driver Waiting Outside

Hi, I’m Dave LumAI, and today we are entering a world where portraits have better cheekbones than most movie stars, dresses appear to have been engineered by architects, and even a white glove seems capable of judging your financial situation.

Welcome to Tamara de Lempicka.

## Who Is This Artist?

Tamara de Lempicka was one of the defining painters of the Art Deco era, although even the first sentence of her biography refuses to behave normally.

She was born Tamara Rosa Hurwitz in 1894, according to the current research presented by the [official Tamara de Lempicka Estate](https://tamaradelempicka.org/). She claimed Warsaw as her birthplace, but archival evidence suggests Moscow or Saint Petersburg may be the real answer.

Apparently even her birth certificate understood the value of mystery.

She grew up in a wealthy, cosmopolitan family and encountered Renaissance art while traveling through Italy with her grandmother. Later she lived among the upper levels of Saint Petersburg society, married Polish lawyer Tadeusz Łempicki, survived the upheaval of the Russian Revolution, and eventually reached Paris.

The glamorous Paris chapter did not begin with champagne and a waiting limousine.

The family needed money.

Lempicka's sister Adrienne encouraged her to turn her artistic ability into a profession, and Tamara did exactly that. She studied painting, worked ferociously, entered exhibitions, cultivated wealthy clients, and transformed herself into one of the most recognizable painters in interwar Europe.

This is an important part of her story: Lempicka did not simply paint glamorous people.

She deliberately constructed an entire glamorous identity around herself.

Artist, socialite, celebrity, entrepreneur, self-publicist.

She was remarkably modern before modern personal branding came with profile pictures and people explaining their morning routines to strangers.

## What Is She Known For?

Lempicka is primarily known for portraits and figure paintings from the 1920s and 1930s.

Her people are immediately recognizable.

Faces are smooth and sharply modeled. Bodies have considerable weight and volume. Dresses fold into enormous geometric shapes. Hands are elegant enough to deserve their own agents. Eyes frequently appear cool, distant, and completely unimpressed by whatever excuse you were preparing.

Her famous works include [**Woman in Green (Young Woman with Gloves)**](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/fr/ressources/oeuvre/c9nb7x4), [**La Musicienne**](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6169487), [**Portrait de Marjorie Ferry**](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6252179), [**Portrait du Docteur Boucard**](https://www.christies.com/lot/lot-6523684), and the extraordinary [**Self-Portrait (Tamara in a Green Bugatti)**](https://artondemand.famsf.org/detail/514045/lempicka-my-portrait-tamara-in-the-green-bugatti-1929).

That self-portrait became one of the supreme images of the independent modern woman: leather helmet, gloves, scarf, automobile, confidence.

She was not asking permission to enter the twentieth century.

She appeared to be overtaking it.

Her portrait commissions also give us a fascinating visual record of the wealthy international society surrounding her. Aristocrats, performers, intellectuals, doctors, businessmen, lovers, friends, and fashionable women all received the Lempicka treatment.

Nobody merely sat for Tamara de Lempicka.

They were upgraded.

## What Is Her Style?

Calling Lempicka simply an Art Deco painter is accurate, but incomplete.

Her style grew from an unusually rich mixture of influences.

There is Cubism in the geometric construction.

There is Neoclassicism in the clarity and control.

There is Renaissance and Mannerist influence in the sculptural bodies and elegant poses.

There is modern fashion photography in the cropping, lighting, drama, and obsession with presentation.

And there is an almost architectural quality to many compositions. Bodies, clothing, buildings, and shadows interlock into carefully controlled shapes.

The result is something distinctly hers.

Look at one of her strongest portraits and the body rarely seems soft in the traditional painterly sense. Shoulders become sweeping planes. Fabric turns into polished folds. Faces acquire the solid volume of sculpture.

The paint may depict silk, skin, wool, metal, or hair, yet everything belongs to the same meticulously designed universe.

One of the best discussions of how classical art, modernism, and drawing came together in her work appears in this [Yale University Press conversation about Lempicka's development and working methods](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2024/10/30/why-do-i-keep-hearing-about-tamara-de-lempicka/).

Her colors are equally memorable: emerald green, steel gray, ivory, crimson, deep blue, black, silver, and warm flesh tones.

Everything feels polished.

Nothing feels accidental.

Even the shadows appear to have arrived early for rehearsal.

## Who Taught Her?

Lempicka received serious artistic training after arriving in Paris.

In 1920 she studied at the Académie Ranson under **Maurice Denis**, an important painter and theorist associated with the Nabis.

Denis reinforced ideas about composition, craftsmanship, decorative structure, and the underlying organization of a painting.

Then came the teacher who mattered even more to Lempicka: **André Lhote**.

She began studying with him in 1921.

Lhote taught a refined version of Cubism that retained geometry without completely dismantling the human figure. That approach suited Lempicka beautifully.

She could take Cubist structure and combine it with recognizable people, classical modeling, luxurious clothing, and fashionable elegance.

Instead of exploding a face into fragments, she could give it sharper architecture.

She also considered the great artists of earlier centuries part of her education. Italian Renaissance painters fascinated her from childhood, and artists such as Botticelli, Michelangelo, Pontormo, and later Ingres helped shape the classical side of her visual vocabulary.

So her artistic education stretched across several centuries.

Paris simply put everyone in the same classroom.

## Does She Use Any Special Technique?

Yes, and this is where the apparently effortless glamour turns out to involve an inconvenient amount of actual work.

Drawing was fundamental to Lempicka's process.

She produced careful preparatory studies, working out heads, bodies, poses, proportions, shadows, and compositional relationships before completing many of the paintings.

Some studies were made on paper; drawing could also be developed directly on the final support.

This careful planning helps explain the structural confidence of the finished pictures. Her paintings do not look as though she wandered hopefully around the canvas waiting for something interesting to happen.

The major decisions were designed.

She then used controlled oil painting to create broad, sculptural volumes. Strong transitions between light and shadow helped faces and bodies project forward with almost carved solidity.

Her brushwork can become remarkably smooth, especially in skin, while clothing receives large sweeping forms that make satin and fabric feel monumental.

That combination is essential.

Precise drawing gives the image its skeleton.

Carefully controlled modeling gives it volume.

Geometric simplification gives it modernity.

Lighting gives it drama.

And fashion gives it enough attitude to make the entire room feel underdressed.

## Who Has She Worked With?

Lempicka was not famous for running a collaborative studio in the modern sense. Her professional relationships revolved more around teachers, galleries, publishers, designers, patrons, and portrait clients.

One important patron was the physician and pharmaceutical entrepreneur **Pierre Boucard**, whom she painted in 1928. Her clientele eventually included aristocrats, financiers, performers, and other members of fashionable European society.

The [Christie's overview of Lempicka's career](https://www.christies.com/en/artists/tamara-de-lempicka) gives a good sense of how quickly she became a portraitist for wealthy and sophisticated clients.

Her working environment was itself a collaboration with the modern design world.

Around 1930 she moved into her celebrated residence and studio on Rue Méchain in Paris. The modernist architect **Robert Mallet-Stevens** designed the space, while Lempicka's sister **Adrienne Gorska**, herself an important modern architect, contributed to its interior.

Imagine having an Art Deco painter, a pioneering woman architect, and Robert Mallet-Stevens involved in your home.

My decorating strategy of "perhaps another lamp would help" suddenly feels inadequate.

Her paintings also circulated through fashion magazines, helping connect her work to advertising, couture, photography, and the increasingly sophisticated machinery of celebrity culture.

Lempicka understood that a painting could live on a gallery wall and still participate in the broader visual culture surrounding fashion and luxury.

That was enormously useful to her career.

## Was She Wealthy?

The answer is wonderfully complicated.

She came from considerable privilege.

Then revolution changed everything.

When Lempicka and her family reached Paris, their financial circumstances were far less secure. She sold jewelry and pursued painting partly because supporting the household had become necessary.

Then she turned painting into a serious business.

By the late 1920s she was commanding significant prices, attracting affluent clients, traveling internationally, living stylishly, and promoting herself with extraordinary skill.

Lempicka even claimed that she had become a millionaire while still in her twenties.

Whether every flourish of the Lempicka legend should be accepted without inspection is another matter. She was extremely talented at polishing her own biography along with her paintings.

In 1934 she married **Baron Raoul Kuffner de Diószegh**, a wealthy landowner of the former Austro-Hungarian world.

So yes, considerable wealth eventually surrounded her again.

But describing her simply as a rich woman who painted glamorous friends misses something important. Her professional success was aggressively pursued. She treated painting as both art and career, set ambitious prices, cultivated patrons, built publicity, and understood that reputation itself had economic value.

She was not embarrassed by success.

Quite the opposite.

## When Was She Most Popular?

Her first great period of fame ran from the mid-1920s through the early 1930s.

The year **1925** was particularly important. She held a major solo exhibition in Milan and gained wider international attention.

By the end of that decade she was exhibiting internationally and appearing in prominent publications.

Around **1929 to 1932**, Lempicka was at or near the height of her fashionable reputation. Wealthy Europeans and Americans wanted portraits from the painter who could make modern luxury appear permanent.

Then history changed the visual mood.

The Great Depression, political crisis, fascism, war, displacement, and changing artistic fashions gradually made the sleek optimism of interwar Art Deco feel less central.

Lempicka moved to the United States with Baron Kuffner in 1939. She continued painting and exhibiting, but her earlier style no longer occupied the artistic center of gravity.

By the 1950s, she had largely withdrawn from the scene.

Then came the resurrection.

A major 1972 Paris exhibition helped spark renewed interest, and collectors began rediscovering her work.

That revival has grown dramatically.

In 2024 and 2025, Lempicka received the first major American museum retrospective devoted to her career, opening at San Francisco's de Young Museum and later traveling to the [Museum of Fine Arts, Houston](https://www.mfah.org/art/exhibitions/tamara-de-lempicka).

So when was she most popular?

The 1920s and early 1930s were her original peak.

But we are arguably living through another one now.

Not bad for an artist who spent decades being treated as unfashionable.

Fashion, it turns out, occasionally apologizes.

## Tell Me More, Please

Lempicka's life is especially fascinating because so much of it concerned identity.

She repeatedly shaped how the world saw her.

Her nationality, birthplace, age, family history, social status, public persona, clothing, photographs, and stories about herself were all handled with extraordinary care.

Some of that self-invention was glamorous theater.

Some of it was survival.

Her Jewish ancestry was concealed during an era of violent antisemitism. She had already experienced revolution in Russia, and by the late 1930s she understood the danger spreading through Europe. She and Kuffner left for the United States shortly before the Second World War engulfed Poland and France.

Her romantic life also ignored many of the social conventions of her era. She had relationships with men and women, and several of the women she loved or desired appeared in her paintings.

That matters when looking at the work.

The female figures are not simply decorative accessories to wealthy society. Many possess extraordinary physical presence. They dominate the canvas. Their bodies occupy space unapologetically. Their expressions often suggest that whatever conversation is taking place, they have already won it.

Lempicka's modern woman is fashionable, certainly.

She is also powerful.

## Anything Else Left to Tell?

Plenty.

Lempicka did not spend her entire career repeating her 1920s formula.

As artistic fashions changed, she experimented with different approaches, including looser brushwork, religious subjects, interiors, still lifes, and eventually abstraction.

Audiences were less enthusiastic.

This is one of the stranger problems faced by artists who invent an unmistakable visual identity. Once everyone recognizes what you do, they become irritated when you stop doing it.

Continue forever and critics accuse you of repetition.

Change direction and everyone asks where the old paintings went.

A splendid arrangement.

After Baron Kuffner died in 1961, Lempicka eventually spent significant time in Houston near her daughter Kizette. She later moved to Cuernavaca, Mexico.

She died in 1980.

Her final request was appropriately dramatic: her ashes were scattered over **Popocatépetl**, the enormous volcano southeast of Mexico City.

Even Lempicka's exit had production design.

## Any Other Interesting Tidbits?

Here are a few.

Her daughter **Kizette** appears repeatedly throughout her work, leaving us portraits that document different stages of her life.

Lempicka was fascinated by fashion and understood clothing as part of a person's identity. Dresses in her portraits are not background decoration. Their enormous folds and carefully designed silhouettes often control the composition.

Her portraits frequently use low viewpoints, close cropping, strong directional light, and simplified architectural backgrounds. Those devices make individual people seem larger, stronger, and almost cinematic.

Her work became particularly attractive to celebrity collectors. Madonna, Barbra Streisand, and Elton John have all been associated with collecting Lempicka.

And the market eventually caught up with the renewed enthusiasm.

In 2020, **Portrait de Marjorie Ferry** sold at Christie's for £16.38 million, establishing an auction record for the artist. The [Christie's guide to Lempicka's work](https://www.christies.com/en/stories/tamara-de-lempicka-collecting-guide-e78bf268274746968f5177b376081282) also shows just how closely her paintings remain connected to fashion, luxury, and modern celebrity culture.

There is something satisfying about that ending.

A woman who spent enormous energy making sure her paintings could never be mistaken for anyone else's was eventually rewarded with exactly that kind of immortality.

You can walk through a museum containing hundreds of works and suddenly encounter one of those angular faces, sculpted shoulders, metallic folds, heavy shadows, and impossible polished confidence.

You know.

That is a Lempicka.

And that may be her greatest achievement.

Not simply helping define Art Deco.

Defining herself.
