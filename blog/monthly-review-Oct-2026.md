# October 2026 Review: The Website Grew, X Fell Off a Cliff, and Sales Remained a Beautiful Fiction

Dave LumAI here, reporting from the monthly analytics department, where optimism is mandatory, mathematics is occasionally optional, and the sales department continues to consist of an empty chair.

October 2026 was an interesting month.

Facebook had a comeback. My website enjoyed some respectable growth. Blog earnings increased despite fewer people reading. X apparently decided that visibility was overrated. And TikTok behaved like someone had unplugged half the internet.

Meanwhile, I kept publishing articles about everything from artificial intelligence to Jeff Wheeler novels, from programming languages to the mysterious disappearance of Git branches that absolutely should have disappeared.

Let's examine what happened, what we learned, and whether November might finally introduce the concept of actual sales.

## First, What Did October Teach Us?

Before drowning in percentages, here are the five questions worth asking.

**1. Can we draw any conclusions from the metrics?**

Yes, although conclusions based on percentage changes without the underlying totals should come with a small warning label.

The most encouraging result was that traffic to LumAIere.com increased by 30%, while Facebook views and reach also grew substantially.

Meanwhile, X and TikTok experienced significant declines.

That suggests my content is performing very differently depending on where people encounter it. There is no universal audience collapse, and no universal success story either.

**2. Are there any patterns?**

Definitely.

Practical technology advice continues to attract attention. Older educational articles remain remarkably durable. Visually interesting content still gets noticed, although not consistently across every platform.

And the relationship between views, engagement, and followers remains about as predictable as a cat choosing where to sleep.

**3. What were my successful actions?**

Continuing to publish useful technical explanations, maintaining an evergreen article library, and creating practical short-form content.

The strongest evidence comes from my website traffic, Facebook's improvement, and the continued popularity of older articles.

**4. What were my unsuccessful actions?**

Turning attention into revenue, for starters.

And whatever I was doing on X and TikTok clearly didn't produce the same results as September.

That doesn't necessarily mean the content was bad. Distribution, audience behavior, and platform algorithms can all influence those numbers.

But pretending those declines don't matter would be an unusually creative approach to analytics.

**5. Any other interesting tidbits?**

One particularly fascinating contradiction: blog earnings increased 15% while views declined 13% and reads dropped 20%.

Apparently, fewer readers can still produce better financial results.

Also, Facebook visits doubled while clicks remained unchanged.

The internet continues to prove that people can be interested in something without behaving the way your analytics spreadsheet expects.

Now, let's get into the numbers.

## Bonus: What Was America's Hottest Political Issue 100 Years Ago?

Travel back exactly a century to the autumn of **1926**.

Calvin Coolidge was president, the Roaring Twenties were in full swing, and Americans were preparing for congressional elections.

And one of the country's biggest political battles concerned something surprisingly ordinary.

**Alcohol.**

Specifically, Prohibition.

The Eighteenth Amendment had established the constitutional foundation for nationwide Prohibition, and the Volstead Act provided its enforcement framework.

The law prohibited the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating alcoholic beverages for ordinary beverage purposes.

But Americans were deeply divided about whether this was sensible public policy.

The debate was commonly framed as **Wets versus Drys**.

The Drys generally supported maintaining Prohibition.

The Wets wanted it modified or repealed.

Supporters argued about public health, morality, family life, and social consequences.

Opponents pointed to bootlegging, organized crime, enforcement failures, and restrictions on personal freedom.

In 1926, this wasn't merely an abstract debate.

It was a real election issue.

A [contemporary October 30, 1926 news report](https://www.upi.com/amp/Archives/1926/10/30/Tuesday-elections-to-decide-32-governorships-may-indicate-presidential-trend/6080913973140/) described multiple state referendums and Senate contests involving Prohibition.

Earlier that year, [TIME reported on the growing political conflict](https://time.com/archive/6663860/prohibition-toil-and-trouble/), particularly in states including Illinois, New York, and Pennsylvania.

Prohibition wasn't the only important controversy. Farm relief, campaign spending, and the Ku Klux Klan were also prominent issues in various races.

But the wet-versus-dry question was certainly among the most recognizable national political battles.

Think about that.

One hundred years ago, Americans were arguing passionately about morality, government authority, personal liberty, and whether the law reflected what people actually wanted.

And politicians were trying to position themselves on both sides of a deeply divisive question.

Some things change.

Others apparently just get faster internet connections.

Prohibition would eventually end nationally in 1933 with the Twenty-first Amendment.

But in November 1926, that resolution was still years away.

History is fascinating partly because the people living through it usually have no idea which controversies future generations will consider completely ridiculous.

I wonder what people in 2126 will think about our arguments today.

Hopefully, they'll at least have figured out how to close browser tabs without starting an argument.
